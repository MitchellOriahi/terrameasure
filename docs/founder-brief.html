01

Where the project stands

The product works. You can open it right now, draw a boundary on any piece of land in the United States, and about a minute later get a verdict on whether that land is worth pursuing, along with the numbers behind it and a link you can send to anyone.

Measuring engine live Works on phones Shareable reports live Accounts live Parcel data: 5 counties Imagery licence to sort Hosting to move

What is left before we put this in front of the public is short, and most of it is decisions and money rather than engineering. That is the honest headline: the hard part, the part that computes an answer out of raw government elevation data and stands behind it with an error margin, is built and tested.

02

What it does, in plain terms

Someone is looking at a piece of land. Before they spend a Saturday driving to it, or thousands of dollars on a surveyor, they want to know one thing: is this worth pursuing. TerraMeasure answers that in about sixty seconds, free, from a phone.

  • A verdict, not a pile of maps. One word: go, proceed with conditions, or not recommended, with one sentence naming the single biggest problem with the site.
  • The ground itself. How steep it is, how much of it is flat enough to build on, how much the land rises and falls, and a 3D model of the site you can spin with a finger.
  • What it would cost to build. How much dirt has to move to level a building pad, and what that costs in dollars. No competitor we have found produces this number at any price.
  • The risks that kill deals. Wetlands, open water and federal flood zones, checked automatically and folded into the verdict rather than left as maps for the buyer to interpret.
  • Proof, not just claims. Every number carries how wrong it could be and names the government dataset it came from. Everything is labelled preliminary and uncertified, because a licensed surveyor still has to seal a real survey.
  • Something to send. Any survey becomes a public link. Whoever opens it needs no account, and can print it or copy it as text into an email.
03

What was finished since the last update

Things people can see

  • Saving a survey now works for everyone, with no account needed. It used to quietly fail.
  • A 3D model of the surveyed ground, spinnable, inside the results and inside every shared report.
  • Every report now names the county and state, and you can retitle it and add your own notes at any time.
  • Reports can be copied as plain text or printed to PDF for a file or a lender.
  • A shared link now previews properly in a text message or an email instead of appearing as a bare address.
  • The front page was rebuilt around a working demo you can drag, rather than a description of the product.

Things that protect us

  • The elevation service was quietly inventing ground where the government data had gaps. It now reports the gap instead. This was the most serious problem found, and it went to the heart of our accuracy claim.
  • Limits added so one runaway script cannot take the service down or get us blocked by the federal data providers.
  • When an outside service fails, the app now says what happened and keeps the user's work, instead of showing an error.
  • The app now updates itself. Returning visitors were being served an old cached copy, so improvements were invisible to them.

The competition, and where we win

The closest name is Acres, built by AcreTrader, which has raised more than 80 million dollars and reports around half a million users. It is worth being clear-eyed: they beat us on breadth of data and on distribution. They do not beat us on the thing we do.

Where we are structurally stronger

  • We answer the question. They hand you layers. Acres gives you ten toggleable maps and leaves the reading to you. That is fine for a professional at a desk and useless to someone with twenty listings open at eight in the evening.
  • Nobody else produces a dollar figure. Not on their free tier, not on their thousand-dollar tier, not in their enterprise beta. What it costs to move the dirt is the number that decides a purchase, and we are the only ones computing it.
  • We publish our error margins. Their own app store reviews complain about acreage being wrong, one user reporting a 250 acre parcel shown as 79. We state how wrong each number could be, which is what makes it usable by someone whose licence is on the line.
  • Free actually means free. Their reviews carry real anger about features moving behind a paywall. Our verdict, numbers, cost range and shareable report are the product, not a trial of it.
  • Nothing to install. Their most common complaint is the app freezing and crashing. We are a web page that works on any phone.

Where they are ahead, honestly

  • Ownership records, sales history and valuations across 150 million parcels. We cover five counties. This is the gap that money closes, and section 06 prices it.
  • Half a million users, funded sales team, apps in both stores.
  • They have an enterprise-only beta that overlaps our territory: slope, buildable area and 3D on a drawn parcel. It is gated behind a sales call today. If they bring it downmarket, our clearest advantage narrows. That is the clock we are running against.
05

What is left before we go public

1. The satellite imagery is not licensed for commercial use

The aerial pictures behind the map come from Esri, whose terms do not permit commercial use without a licence. This is the one item that could actually stop a launch, and it is cheap to fix: swapping to Mapbox costs nothing until we pass 50,000 map views a month.

Decision needed: approve the swap. About a day of work.

2. Hosting moves to DigitalOcean

We are on a free hosting tier that puts the service to sleep after fifteen minutes of quiet, so the first visitor after a lull waits up to a minute. That is our worst first impression. You already use DigitalOcean, so we move there.

Decision needed: which DigitalOcean account to bill it to. Costs are in section 06.

3. Parcel data coverage

Today we read parcel records straight from five county governments, free. Everywhere else, the user draws the boundary by hand and gets the identical analysis. Nationwide coverage means paying a data vendor, and that is the single largest cost in this document.

Decision needed: whether nationwide parcels are a launch requirement or a follow-up.

4. We currently keep no record of the surveys we run

Every survey and its prediction is discarded unless the user saves it to their own phone. Your build spec calls the accuracy feedback loop our long-term moat, and it cannot start until we are recording predictions. Every week we run without this is data we cannot get back. It costs nothing but a few days of work.

Recommendation: build it before launch, not after.

Two things make that number smaller. First, we do not use the map tiles that make up most of a parcel subscription, only the records, so we should not pay for a tile-heavy plan. Second, we currently look up the same parcel again every time somebody taps it. Remembering results for a few weeks is a couple of days of work and should cut that 800 dollar line by a third to a half. Regrid publishes no base price without an account, and offers a free 30 day trial, which is the cheapest way to get the real figure and test the integration.
